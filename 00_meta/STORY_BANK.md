## 2026-08-13 — Childhood: fixing broken devices

**Raw:** At 12-13, started opening broken devices with no training or anyone around who knew this stuff, trying to make them work again. Sometimes succeeded, sometimes not. Recurring detail: there would almost always be leftover screws he could never place — but the device would still work, still perform its intended task, missing screws and all.

**Reflection (his own, in the moment):** Looking back, it reads as a metaphor — things don't need to be perfect to work and provide the value they're meant to provide. Chasing perfection is often really a way to avoid moving forward, or to avoid exposing yourself.

**Possible fit:** Origin-story material for Chapter 1 (Why AI Engineering, Why Now) — establishes lifelong pull toward technology/building value, and the "leftover screws" image could work as a recurring motif for the book's whole ship-imperfect-but-working ethos. Story is continuing across multiple messages — more to come before finalizing placement.

## 2026-08-13 — University: technical degree, hardware to software, tools as means not identity

**Raw:** Studied science/technology, joined a technical university. Started with more hardware experience; interest in software grew from there — began by writing software *for* hardware, then drifted further into software over time, but "never in a standard way." Was clear early on he didn't want to become a "standard developer." The real interest was always technology, programming, and building things — but most of all, solving problems. Programming itself was never the interest; the interest was in learning whatever tools let him solve problems.

**Reflection (his own, in the moment):** Frames programming/tools as instrumental, not identity — a means to solve problems, not the point itself.

**Possible fit:** Continues the Chapter 1 origin arc — sets up the "tool-agnostic problem solver" identity that explains later career moves (hardware → software → founder → now AI engineering) as one continuous thread rather than a pivot. Likely pairs with the childhood entry above. More to come.

## 2026-08-13 — Moving to Vienna as an Erasmus student, on intuition alone

**Raw:** Calls himself a technical person but leans heavily on intuition — the biggest example being the decision to move to Vienna, Austria. Didn't feel he fit the standard path available in Portugal: finish university, take a junior role at a big company, slowly climb the ladder over years. That wasn't his way of going through life, and he didn't see room to do it differently there. So he left, as an Erasmus student — despite not speaking German, despite most studies being conducted in German, despite financial constraints, and with no community or support system waiting for him. No real plan — just the belief/dream that there was potential somewhere else to build his own path.

**Reflection (his own, in the moment):** Frames the decision as illogical on paper (language, money, no plan, no support) but right on intuition — a pattern of choosing an unclear but self-directed path over a clear but standard one.

**Possible fit:** Strong Chapter 1 material — extends the "not a standard developer" thread from the university entry into "not a standard career path" more broadly, and sets up the founder/AskDila arc that presumably follows. The intuition-over-logic framing could become a second recurring motif alongside "imperfect but working" (leftover screws). More to come.

## 2026-08-13 — Software tester → test automation → Scrum Master/agile coach

**Raw:** First professional role was software tester — felt like the right fit at the time: working directly with technology but not as a developer, still able to see how things worked, support real products, watch real people use them, and understand how people actually use tools. Did this for some years, moving into test automation along the way. Eventually hit a point with no clear growth path from there. Discovered Scrum Master / agile coach roles — a way to shape how technology gets built without directly writing code, testing, or owning product decisions: shaping the process so people could better contribute their own knowledge/experience toward more meaningful solutions.

**Reflection (his own, in the moment):** Frames each career move as chasing a closer relationship to real impact and to *how* people build/use things, not chasing a title — tester → automation → agile coaching as steps toward influencing how good products get made, not just testing them.

**Possible fit:** Continues the Chapter 1 arc — builds the professional throughline (adjacent-to-code roles, always oriented toward real impact and enabling others) that presumably leads into founding AskDila/B-Software. More to come.

## 2026-08-13 — 10 years in IT, breadth across industries/products, and burnout

**Raw:** Worked across several industries and product types — B2B, B2C, B2B2C, pure software and software for hardware. Involved in product decisions, product design, product development. Always had a strong parallel interest in business strategy and how businesses work. Ten years in the IT industry total, always highly motivated to learn and provide more value — learned a lot, grew a lot. But also learned this specific path (the all-in, always-more-motivated mode) wasn't sustainable for him: gave too much of himself — energy and commitment — and it led to burnout. He's explicit that there are multiple valid ways to read it (system being broken vs. not knowing his own boundaries), but chooses to see it as something that had to happen for him to stop, realign, and define his own direction.

**Reflection (his own, in the moment):** Deliberately avoids assigning blame (system vs. self) and instead frames the burnout as a necessary forcing function for redirection — consistent with the earlier "doesn't need to be perfect to work" and intuition-over-standard-path motifs already logged.

**Possible fit:** Likely pivot point in the Chapter 1 arc — probably where the story turns toward founding AskDila/B-Software and, later, toward why AI engineering specifically (a redefinition of direction post-burnout). Handle with care in the actual chapter draft — this is personal and should stay in his own words/framing, not be smoothed over into a generic "hustle culture bad" narrative. More to come.

## 2026-08-13 — Burnout's lesson, the burnout-coach detour, and the pivot to AI

**Raw:** The burnout taught him to follow his gut and listen to his body. The forced break taught him to define what he actually wanted to do and what value he wanted to give the world — explicitly not about getting rich, about helping people. Initial plan post-burnout: become a burnout-prevention coach for IT companies, bringing his own field experience to help them reshape how they work. Hit two concrete walls in Austria: (1) heavy bureaucracy — to legally offer that kind of service he'd essentially need to be a therapist; (2) for companies to pay for it, they'd need to want to change their traditional way of working from within, or have internal people pushing for that change — which is rare/difficult to happen. Meanwhile AI usage was growing exponentially. Because of his underlying interest in technology and solving problems, it became natural to want to find problems to solve using this emerging technology.

**Reflection (his own, in the moment):** Frames the AI pivot not as trend-chasing but as the same lifelong pattern (find problems, use whatever tool solves them) redirected once the burnout-coach path hit real structural walls (regulation, market willingness).

**Possible fit:** The key hinge of Chapter 1 — directly explains "why AI engineering" as a continuation of his actual values (helping people, not getting rich) after a more direct path (burnout coaching) proved structurally blocked. Still need: the AskDila/B-Software founding story itself, and the concrete "why now" trigger. More to come.

## 2026-08-13 — AskDila's mission, the app-vs-production-ready gap, and "why now"

**Raw:** AI is accessible to (almost) everyone, often free or low-cost — but not everyone has the technical understanding to actually benefit from it. Decided to use AI to create value specifically for non-technical businesses that matter to society and will keep existing regardless of how much technology advances. AskDila exists to let small businesses focus on what they're good at, by using AI to take over repeatable work that isn't core to their passion. To do that for real, he had to learn to build real products with AI — and drew a sharp distinction: having an application running is not the same as having a *production-ready* application — reliable, safe, scalable. That gap is what forced him to actually become a real AI engineer, not just someone who calls an API. On "why now": "why not now" — burnout gave him the space to stop and reflect, and the work he did on himself gave him the courage to pivot and pursue his own dreams.

**Reflection (his own, in the moment):** Explicitly names the running-app vs. production-ready-app distinction as the reason the skill-building (this whole roadmap/book) is necessary at all — not a nice-to-have. "Why now" is answered as personal readiness (post-burnout courage), not market timing.

**Possible fit:** Closes the Chapter 1 arc — this is the direct answer to the chapter's own title. The "running app vs. production-ready app" line is strong and could double as a thesis statement for the whole book, not just this chapter. Story arc now feels complete: childhood → university → Vienna → career breadth → burnout → burnout-coach detour → AI/AskDila pivot → why now. Market-research "why now" (job market data, salary tiers) can layer on top of this personal "why now" in The Concept/Debrief beats rather than replacing it.
